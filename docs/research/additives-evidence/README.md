## Summary

# Summary

- IARC classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic (Group 2B) in 2023 while JECFA reaffirmed its ADI of 0–40 mg/kg body weight [S98].
- WHO suggests that non-sugar sweeteners not be used for weight control or to reduce noncommunicable disease risk, as a conditional recommendation [S92].
- NutriNet-Santé cohorts link artificial sweeteners to cancer, cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes [S66] [S273] [S348], emulsifiers to cardiovascular disease, cancer and type 2 diabetes [S78] [S65] [S382], and additive nitrites to type 2 diabetes [S32].
- Higher circulating erythritol and xylitol were associated with major adverse cardiovascular events and enhanced thrombosis [S121] [S302], while EFSA set an erythritol ADI of 0.5 grams per kilogram of body weight per day based on its laxative effect [S192].
- EFSA concluded titanium dioxide (E171) can no longer be considered safe as a food additive [S68], and the FDA revoked FD&C Red No. 3 under the Delaney Clause [S373].
- IARC classifies processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) [S134], and EFSA found that nitrosamines in food raise a health concern for all age groups [S19].
- EFSA withdrew the temporary ADI for sulfites and found a safety concern [S24], while it found no safety concern for celluloses [S155] and mono- and di-glycerides of fatty acids [S226].

## Report

# Food additives: official positions and human evidence (R1)

> [model-hint] This dossier covers sweeteners, polyols, emulsifiers, colours, nitrites/nitrates and other preservatives or additives. Evidence is graded from strongest to weakest as official assessment, meta-analysis, human trial, prospective cohort, animal study and in vitro study. Cohort associations, including the many NutriNet-Santé analyses, do not establish causation.

## 1. Intense sweeteners

### Aspartame (E951)

- In July 2023 IARC classified aspartame as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B), on the basis of limited evidence for cancer in humans, specifically hepatocellular carcinoma [S98].
- The IARC Monographs working group published its summary of the aspartame evaluation in The Lancet Oncology in 2023 [S383].
- JECFA concluded that the data gave no sufficient reason to change the acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0–40 mg/kg body weight for aspartame and reaffirmed it [S98].
- The FDA disagrees with IARC's conclusion that these studies support classifying aspartame as a possible carcinogen to humans [S27].
- The FDA ADI for aspartame is 50 mg/kg bw/d [S27].
- EFSA lists its re-evaluation of aspartame (E 951) as completed in 2013 [S202].

### All intense sweeteners: WHO and FDA

- The 2023 WHO guideline states: "WHO suggests that non-sugar sweeteners not be used as a means of achieving weight control or reducing the risk of noncommunicable diseases (conditional recommendation)" [S92].
- WHO explains that the recommendation is conditional, so policy decisions based on it may need substantive discussion in each country [S360].
- The FDA lists six high-intensity sweeteners approved as food additives: saccharin, aspartame, acesulfame potassium, sucralose, neotame and advantame [S72].
- For certain highly purified steviol glycosides, the FDA has not questioned the notifiers' GRAS determinations under the intended conditions of use [S72].

### Human cohorts (NutriNet-Santé) and meta-analysis

- In 102,865 French adults of NutriNet-Santé, total artificial sweetener intake was associated with overall cancer (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.25), as were aspartame (HR 1.15) and acesulfame-K (HR 1.13) [S66].
- In the same cohort, total artificial sweetener intake was associated with cardiovascular disease (HR 1.09, 1.01 to 1.18) and cerebrovascular disease (HR 1.18, 1.06 to 1.31) [S273].
- Aspartame was associated with cerebrovascular events (HR 1.17), and acesulfame potassium (HR 1.40) and sucralose (HR 1.31) with coronary heart disease [S273].
- For type 2 diabetes, higher consumers of artificial sweeteners had a higher risk than non-consumers (HR 1.69, 95% CI 1.45-1.97) [S348].
- The type 2 diabetes associations held for aspartame (HR 1.63), acesulfame-K (HR 1.70) and sucralose (HR 1.34) [S348].
- The authors of that analysis state that the potential for reverse causality cannot be eliminated [S348].
- A meta-analysis of longitudinal cohort studies found a pooled HR of 1.31 (95% CI 1.16–1.49) for type 2 diabetes with higher artificial sweetener intake, with substantial heterogeneity [S351].

### Acesulfame K (E950)

- In its re-evaluation the EFSA FAF Panel set an ADI of 15 mg/kg bw per day for acesulfame K, based on the highest dose tested without adverse effects in a chronic rat study [S31].
- EFSA found no safety concerns for genotoxicity of acesulfame K and its degradation products [S31].
- For the impurity 5-chloro-acesulfame, EFSA identified a genotoxicity concern in silico and recommended a maximum limit of 0.1 mg/kg [S31].

### Sucralose (E955) and sucralose-6-acetate

- In its re-evaluation, EFSA found no genotoxicity concerns for sucralose (E 955) or its impurities and degradation products [S18].
- EFSA concluded there was no need to revise the sucralose ADI of 15 mg/kg bw per day and no safety concern at the reported uses and use levels [S18].
- EFSA could not conclude on the safety of a proposed extension of sucralose use to fine bakery wares, because of uncertainties about chlorinated compounds that may form during baking [S18].
- In in vitro screening assays, Schiffman and colleagues found that sucralose-6-acetate, an impurity and intermediate of sucralose manufacture, was genotoxic and clastogenic [S224].
- The same study reported that commercial sucralose samples contained up to 0.67% sucralose-6-acetate [S224].

### Saccharin (E954), steviol glycosides (E960), cyclamate (E952)

- In 2024 EFSA set an ADI of 9 mg/kg bw per day for saccharin and its salts, using decreased body weight in animal studies as the endpoint [S6].
- In 2024 EFSA concluded there was insufficient justification to increase the ADI of 4 mg/kg bw per day for steviol glycosides, expressed as steviol equivalents [S132].
- A review reports that cyclamate is approved in the EU and over 100 other countries, with ADIs of 0–11 mg/kg/d from JECFA and 0–7 mg/kg/d from the SCF [S49].
- EFSA lists the re-evaluation of cyclamates (E 952) as ongoing [S202].

## 2. Erythritol, xylitol and other polyols

- In a discovery cohort of 1,157 patients and two validation cohorts, higher circulating erythritol was associated with 3-year major adverse cardiovascular events (fourth versus first quartile HR 1.80 and 2.21) [S121].
- In the same study, erythritol enhanced platelet reactivity in vitro and thrombosis in vivo, and erythritol ingestion in 8 healthy volunteers raised plasma levels for more than 2 days [S121].
- A Mendelian randomization study found no supportive evidence that erythritol increases coronary artery disease or cardiometabolic disease [S385].
- A narrative review notes that patients with chronically elevated erythritol from inborn errors of metabolism do not exhibit higher platelet activation or thrombosis risk [S43].
- In cultured human brain microvascular endothelial cells, 6 mM erythritol for 3 h increased reactive oxygen species production [S230].
- In 2023 EFSA set an ADI for erythritol of 0.5 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, to protect against its laxative effect [S192].
- EFSA estimated that acute and chronic exposure to erythritol is above this ADI in all population groups [S192].
- Fasting plasma xylitol was associated with major adverse cardiovascular events in a validation cohort (third versus first tertile HR 1.57, 1.12-2.21) [S302].
- The same study reported that xylitol enhanced platelet reactivity and thrombosis potential, including in an intervention in healthy volunteers [S302].
- A systematic review found that polyols are malabsorbed in a dose-dependent fashion in healthy individuals, with conflicting results in patients with irritable bowel syndrome [S384].
- A Cochrane review of 10 studies found only low-quality evidence that fluoride toothpaste containing xylitol may prevent caries better than fluoride-only toothpaste in children [S237].
- EFSA lists the re-evaluations of sorbitols (E 420), mannitol (E 421), isomalt (E 953), maltitols (E 965), lactitols (E 966) and xylitol (E 967) as ongoing [S202].

## 3. Emulsifiers

- In NutriNet-Santé adults, higher intake of celluloses (E460-E468) was associated with cardiovascular disease (HR 1.05 per SD), including cellulose E460 and carboxymethylcellulose E466 (HR 1.03 per SD) [S78].
- In the same analysis, higher intakes of mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471 and E472) were associated with higher risks of all cardiovascular outcomes [S78].
- Trisodium phosphate (E339) was associated with coronary heart disease (HR 1.06) [S78].
- In a NutriNet-Santé cancer analysis of 92,000 adults, mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471) were associated with overall cancer (HR 1.15), breast cancer (HR 1.24) and prostate cancer (HR 1.46) [S65].
- In that analysis, carrageenan (E407) was associated with breast cancer (HR 1.28), and no emulsifier was associated with colorectal cancer [S65].
- In NutriNet-Santé adults, total carrageenans, carrageenan gum (E407), tripotassium phosphate (E340), E472e and sodium citrate (E331) were associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes [S382].
- In a double-blind controlled-feeding study, healthy adults given 15 g per day of carboxymethylcellulose for 11 days had modestly more postprandial abdominal discomfort and reduced gut microbiota diversity compared with controls [S143].
- In mice, carboxymethylcellulose and polysorbate-80 induced low-grade inflammation and obesity/metabolic syndrome and promoted colitis in predisposed animals [S107].
- In a small randomized trial in ulcerative colitis patients in remission, three patients receiving carrageenan capsules relapsed and none receiving placebo did [S222].
- In a small randomized feeding trial in prediabetes, participants on a carrageenan-free diet had declines in HbA1c and HOMA-IR [S236].
- In 2018 EFSA concluded that the existing group ADI of 75 mg/kg bw per day for carrageenan (E 407) and processed Eucheuma seaweed (E 407a) should be considered temporary [S127].
- In 2015 EFSA allocated a group ADI of 25 mg/kg bw/day to polysorbates, including polysorbate 80 (E 433) [S89].
- In 2018 EFSA concluded there was no need for a numerical ADI and no safety concern for unmodified and modified celluloses, including E 460 and E 466 [S155].
- In 2017 EFSA concluded there was no need for a numerical ADI and no safety concern for mono- and di-glycerides of fatty acids (E 471) at reported uses [S226].

## 4. Colours

### The Southampton six (E102, E104, E110, E122, E124, E129)

- In the 2007 Southampton randomised double-blind trial, a mix of artificial colours and sodium benzoate (mix A) increased global hyperactivity in 3-year-old children compared with placebo (effect size 0.20) [S153].
- In 8/9-year-old children, mix A and mix B both had adverse effects in the restricted analysis, and the authors concluded that artificial colours or sodium benzoate (or both) increased hyperactivity [S153].
- EFSA judged in 2008 that the study gave limited evidence of a small effect on activity and attention in some children, not consistent across age groups and mixtures [S133].
- Under EU Regulation 1333/2008, foods containing Sunset yellow (E 110), Quinoline yellow (E 104), Carmoisine (E 122), Allura red (E 129), Tartrazine (E 102) or Ponceau 4R (E 124) must carry the statement "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children" [S169].
- In 2009 EFSA lowered the ADIs for Quinoline Yellow (E104), Sunset Yellow FCF (E110) and Ponceau 4R (E124) and did not change those for Tartrazine (E102), Azorubine/Carmoisine (E122) and Allura Red AC (E129) [S201].
- EFSA's 2009 ADI for Quinoline Yellow is 0.5 mg/kg bw/day [S332].
- EFSA's 2009 ADI for Ponceau 4R is 0.7 mg/kg bw/day [S272].
- EFSA set a temporary ADI of 1 mg/kg bw/day for Sunset Yellow FCF [S285].
- EFSA found no reason to revise the Tartrazine ADI of 7.5 mg/kg bw/day [S240].
- EFSA found no reason to revise the Azorubine/Carmoisine ADI of 4 mg/kg bw/day [S340].
- EFSA found no reason to revise the Allura Red AC ADI of 7 mg/kg bw/day [S149].
- Following EFSA's lowered ADI for Quinoline Yellow, Commission Regulation 232/2012 amended its conditions of use and use levels [S324].
- In 2021 California's OEHHA found that consumption of synthetic food dyes can result in hyperactivity and other neurobehavioural problems in some children, and that current federal levels for safe intake may not sufficiently protect children [S183].

### Titanium dioxide (E171)

- In 2021 EFSA concluded that titanium dioxide can no longer be considered safe as a food additive, because genotoxicity concerns could not be excluded [S68].
- As a result, EFSA stated that an ADI cannot be established for E171 [S68].
- Commission Regulation (EU) 2022/63 amended the EU food additive annexes as regards titanium dioxide (E 171) [S91].
- Ireland's food safety authority states that the transitional period ended on 7 August 2022, after which food could no longer contain titanium dioxide [S45].

### Erythrosine (E127, FD&C Red No. 3)

- The FDA revoked the authorization of FD&C Red No. 3 in food and ingested drugs under the Delaney Clause, citing cancer in male rats through a rat-specific hormonal mechanism that does not occur in humans [S373].
- Manufacturers have until January 15, 2027 (food) or January 18, 2028 (ingested drugs) to reformulate [S373].
- In 2011 EFSA kept the erythrosine ADI of 0.1 mg/kg bw/day and considered the rat thyroid tumours secondary to effects on thyroid function [S81].

## 5. Nitrites and nitrates (E249–E252)

- In 2015 IARC classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), based on sufficient evidence that it causes colorectal cancer [S134].
- IARC estimated that each 50 gram portion of processed meat eaten daily increases colorectal cancer risk by 18% [S134].
- IARC classifies ingested nitrate or nitrite under conditions that result in endogenous nitrosation as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A) [S375].
- In 2017 EFSA set an ADI for nitrite of 0.07 mg/kg bw per day and found no in vivo genotoxic potential for sodium and potassium nitrite [S90].
- EFSA kept the nitrate ADI of 3.7 mg/kg bw per day and found the carcinogenicity studies of nitrate in mice and rats negative [S173].
- In its 2023 opinion, EFSA's CONTAM Panel concluded that the margin of exposure for carcinogenic N-nitrosamines in food is below 10,000 for all age groups, which raises a health concern [S19].
- EFSA identified 'meat and meat products' as the main food category contributing to this nitrosamine exposure [S19].
- In 2022 ANSES confirmed an association between colorectal cancer risk and exposure to nitrites and/or nitrates, whether from processed meat or drinking water [S67].
- ANSES advises limiting delicatessen meat to 150g per week [S67].
- The EU has lowered the maximum levels of nitrites and nitrates that may be used as food additives [S209].
- In NutriNet-Santé adults, high consumers of additive nitrates had higher breast cancer risk (HR 1.24) and high consumers of additive nitrites had higher prostate cancer risk (HR 1.58) [S157].
- No association with cancer was observed for nitrates and nitrites from natural sources [S157].
- In 104,168 NutriNet-Santé adults, higher exposure to additive nitrites and sodium nitrite (E250) was associated with type 2 diabetes (HR 1.53 and 1.54), with no association for nitrates [S32].
- A 2026 NutriNet-Santé analysis associated sodium nitrite with prostate cancer (HR 1.32) and potassium nitrate with overall cancer (HR 1.13) and breast cancer (HR 1.22) [S17].
- In the EPIC cohort, dietary nitrosyl-heme from processed meats was not associated with colorectal cancer risk (HR 1.01) [S221].

## 6. Other preservatives and additives

### Benzoates (E210–E213)

- In 2016 EFSA set a group ADI of 5 mg/kg bw per day for benzoic acid and its salts, and found it exceeded in toddlers and children who regularly drink flavoured drinks in the brand-loyal scenario [S162].
- The Southampton trial challenge drinks combined sodium benzoate with the artificial colours [S153].

### Sulfites (E220–E228)

- In its follow-up opinion EFSA withdrew the temporary group ADI for sulfur dioxide–sulfites because the toxicity database was inadequate, and used a margin of exposure approach instead [S24].
- EFSA concluded that estimated dietary exposures to sulfites raise a safety concern [S24].
- In NutriNet-Santé, total sulfites (HR 1.12) and potassium metabisulfite (HR 1.11) were associated with overall cancer [S17].
- In NutriNet-Santé, potassium metabisulfite was among the preservatives associated with higher type 2 diabetes incidence [S262].

### Phosphates (E338–E452)

- In 2019 EFSA derived a group ADI for phosphates of 40 mg/kg bw per day, expressed as phosphorus [S301].
- EFSA noted that estimated exposure exceeded this ADI for infants, toddlers and other children at the mean level [S301].
- A review in a German medical journal states that inorganic phosphate in food additives is effectively absorbed and can raise serum phosphate in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease [S188].

### Glutamates (E621)

- In 2017 EFSA set a group ADI of 30 mg/kg body weight per day for glutamic acid and glutamates [S379].
- EFSA found that exposure may exceed not only this level but also doses associated with adverse effects in humans for some population groups [S379].
- In a multicentre double-blind placebo-controlled study, large doses of MSG given without food elicited more symptoms than placebo in self-reported sensitive people, but responses were not consistent on retesting [S268].

### BHA (E320) and BHT (E321)

- In 2011 EFSA established an ADI of 1.0 mg/kg bw/day for BHA [S372].
- IARC classified BHA as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B), based on sufficient evidence in experimental animals [S372].
- The US National Toxicology Program lists BHA as reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen, based on animal studies [S113].
- EFSA raised the BHT ADI to 0.25 mg/kg bw/day and concluded BHT is not of concern for genotoxicity [S377].

### Preservative mixtures in NutriNet-Santé

- In 108,723 participants, potassium sorbate, potassium metabisulfite, sodium nitrite and other preservatives were associated with higher type 2 diabetes incidence [S262].
- In NutriNet-Santé participants, total non-antioxidant preservatives were associated with hypertension (HR 1.29) and cardiovascular disease (HR 1.16) [S5].
- Germany's BfR cautions that the NutriNet-Santé preservative-cancer study is observational and that its associations cannot automatically be interpreted as causal [S371].

## 7. Context: ultra-processed foods

- An umbrella review of 45 pooled analyses found direct associations between ultra-processed food exposure and 32 health parameters [S243].
- In a randomized inpatient trial in 20 adults, an ultra-processed diet increased energy intake by 508 kcal/day compared with an unprocessed diet [S278].

## Open questions and limitations

> [model-hint] Most human signals for additives come from a single French cohort (NutriNet-Santé) and await replication elsewhere. Erythritol and xylitol findings rely on circulating levels in patients at cardiovascular risk, where endogenous production may confound the link. Several EFSA re-evaluations (polyols, cyclamates) are still ongoing, so the official picture for those additives is incomplete.
